01 / Perspective

From inspiration
to investigation.

Spacetime brings Terence McKenna’s Timewave Zero into conversation with Alexander Chizhevsky’s historical hypotheses about the Sun and collective human activity.

The starting question is open: do mathematical patterns help explain anything in measured solar activity or human change?

The VICE article Is the Solar Cycle Stoking ISIS? is part of the creative inspiration. These ideas remain hypotheses.

02 / Interpretation

Connection, change
and human flourishing.

Novelty, progress and entropy describe different things. McKenna used novelty for complexity and connectedness. In the historical Timewave, a lower curve means greater novelty.

Human evolution appears as sourced event annotations. The indicator menu adds global measures of economic growth, human development, emotional wellbeing, life satisfaction and conflict, one series at a time.

Sequence entropy measures uncertainty in a sequence window. It is not a measurement of human emotion, social disorder or thermodynamic entropy.

03 / Open methods

Inspect the mathematics.

The shared clock

One Fibonacci value and one King Wen hexagram occupy one shared step. Their position counters realign after 192 steps: eight Fibonacci cycles and three King Wen traversals. Changing the viewing window leaves the clock and origin fixed.

The default origin is 1 January 2000 at 00:00 UTC, a declared reference convention. Day steps use elapsed 24-hour intervals. Month and year steps use calendar boundaries, with a fractional position inside each interval; decade steps are ten calendar years. This calendar clock is not a claim of a universal physical time quantum.

Timewave Zero keeps its own historical clock: one day per line, 384 seed values, scale factor 64 and zero point 21 December 2012 at 06:00 in Colombia (11:00 UTC). Shared-clock controls affect the Fibonacci and King Wen layers only. Observations retain their original dates and frequency.

Fibonacci sequence & King Wen sequence

Fibonacci: F₀ = 0, F₁ = 1, Fₙ₊₂ = (Fₙ₊₁ + Fₙ) mod 9. The complete state repeats after 24 steps. We plot native residues 0–8; the Atlas’s display convention represents residue 0 as 9.

King Wen: keep the traditional order of 64 hexagrams. Encode yang as 1 and yin as 0, with the bottom line carrying weight 2⁰. Reduce the six-bit integer modulo 9. This is a new experimental representation, distinct from McKenna’s original derivation.

Neighbouring hexagram line-change counts range from 0 to 6; reducing those counts modulo 9 leaves them unchanged. The downloadable view retains all 64 binary patterns, integers, residues and line-change counts.

Modern sequence methods

Sequence values: piecewise-linear interpolation between adjacent residues, with periodic wrap. Interpolation is a drawing and modelling convention.

Change between steps: the absolute difference between consecutive residues, held for the current step.

Multiscale transform: a finite weighted sum of the same periodic sequence at scales 1, 2, 4 and 8. At depth d, W(x) = Σ(k=0…d−1) 2⁻ᵏL(2ᵏx) / Σ(k=0…d−1) 2⁻ᵏ, where L is the interpolated sequence. This finite construction is separate from the original Timewave function.

Information entropy: Shannon H = −Σ p log₂ p in the trailing 12 sequence positions, including the current position, using cyclic wrap. Units are bits. These methods describe the sequences; predictive validity for human or solar outcomes has not been established.

Timewave Zero: original research preset

The historical layer ports Peter Meyer’s published point evaluator with the Kelley DATA.TW1 seed, including its value 22 at index 119. It retains scale factor 64 and the Apple II normalization by 64³. It is numerically checked against the public-domain C implementation.

The graph preserves the original orientation: lower values correspond to greater novelty in McKenna’s interpretation. Values after the historical zero point are left undefined. The preset opens 1945–2012; it does not move the endpoint to the present.

Reproducing the function establishes mathematical fidelity. It does not validate the historical interpretation or the 2012 prediction.

Observations, coverage & future layers

Sunspot number: WDC-SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium, Brussels, version 2.0. Annual observations cover 1700–2025; monthly observations cover 1749–2025 in this snapshot, retrieved 10 November 2026. Windows of at most 30 years use monthly observations where available; wider windows use annual means. Early annual values have sparse coverage. Missing values remain missing; sunspot number is an index.

Data are redistributed for this research use under CC BY-NC 4.0. Changes: parsed for display and clipped to complete years through 2025. The exported view records the cadence and provenance. SILSO source and documentation.

Solar cycles vary. We retain observed dates and amplitudes instead of imposing a perfect 11-year sine wave. Millennial views need a separately documented proxy reconstruction and uncertainty model.

Ionospheric total electron content, geomagnetic indices and Schumann resonances require their own location, cadence, calibration and quality controls. No measurements for these layers are loaded yet.

Open Data Cube is an open-source Earth-observation data platform supported by organisations including CSIRO. The holding link does not imply a connected weather or hyperspectral feed.

Event markers are a small illustrative selection with source links, not a representative sample or a scored measure of progress. Future comparisons need predefined outcomes, phase and scale controls, autocorrelation-aware baselines and a held-out test period.

Human indicators: definitions & coverage

The five human-indicator categories use published global aggregates, with annual observations placed at midyear. US equity data are monthly and placed at the middle of each month. Connecting lines guide the eye; inspection returns the value for the selected year or month. Missing years remain empty. Changing the sequence clock never resamples these observations. The export retains the source, units, coverage and original rows.

Economic growth: World Bank annual real GDP growth, 1961–2025. Human development: UNDP’s world HDI, 1990–2023, combining health, education and income. World Bank · UNDP.

Emotional wellbeing: Gallup’s global Positive and Negative Experience Indexes, 2006–2025. They describe reported daily experiences, aggregated annually. Life satisfaction: Gallup’s global percentage classified as thriving, 2006–2024: current life rated at least 7/10 and expected life in five years at least 8/10. This is distinct from the World Happiness Report’s mean life-evaluation score and its three-year ranking averages. The published tables provide no interval estimates. Gallup emotions · Gallup life evaluation.

Conflict: summed battle-related deaths in UCDP state-based conflicts, 1989–2025, version 26.1. Separate non-state and one-sided violence datasets are excluded. Vertical ranges sum the source’s low and high estimates; these are not statistical confidence intervals. Cite Davies, Pettersson and Öberg (2026), Organized violence 1989–2025, and violent political protests, Journal of Peace Research. Paper · UCDP data.

Sources retrieved 10 November 2026. World Bank and UCDP: CC BY 4.0. UNDP: CC BY 3.0 IGO. Gallup: published aggregate statistics with attribution. Coverage, survey composition and revisions differ between sources; alignment alone establishes no causal relationship.

Markets, social emotion & research references

S&P 500: Robert Shiller’s S&P Composite history supplies 1,860 monthly average prices from January 1871 through December 2025. Before the modern index launched in March 1957, the record uses predecessor US equity series. Select nominal price, monthly price return or trailing 12-month volatility. Shiller data and methodology · Index history.

Monthly price return = 100 × (Pₜ / Pₜ₋₁ − 1), using monthly average prices and excluding dividends. Volatility = sample standard deviation of 12 monthly percentage changes × √12; the first value follows 12 valid changes. These measures differ from month-end returns and volatility calculated from daily prices. Trading volume is absent from this dataset.

GDP measures economic production. Stock-market returns, trading volume and volatility describe different aspects of market behaviour. Fibonacci retracement measures price pullbacks between selected highs and lows; the repeating Fibonacci sequence on this page is a separate mathematical model.

Siganos, Vagenas-Nanos and Verwijmeren (2014), Facebook’s daily sentiment and international stock markets, Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization: Facebook sentiment across 20 markets was associated with returns, volume and volatility, including a Sunday-to-Monday relationship.

Li, Shen, Xue and Zhang (2017), Daily happiness and stock returns: The case of Chinese company listed in the United States, Economic Modelling: Twitter-derived happiness had contemporaneous and lead–lag relationships with returns, volatility and trading volume.

Bollen, Mao and Zeng (2011), Twitter mood predicts the stock market, Journal of Computational Science: selected Twitter mood measures improved Dow Jones forecasts in the study’s sample and test setup.

These studies motivate questions about sentiment and markets. They do not establish solar or Fibonacci effects. Predictive research needs a fixed observation clock, timestamp-aware data, predefined tests, held-out periods and comparisons with simple baselines. A lead–lag association alone does not demonstrate a causal mechanism or durable trading performance.
