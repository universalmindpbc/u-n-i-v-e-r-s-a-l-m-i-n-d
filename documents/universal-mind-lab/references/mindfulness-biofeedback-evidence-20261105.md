# Mindfulness Biofeedback / AR / VR / Mixed Reality

**Universal Mind Lab road train · Research discussion · 5 November 2026**

Can a young person learn to regulate their breathing or stress by seeing and hearing their own physiology change the room? Which benefits come from the practice, which come from live feedback, and which survive beyond the session? Those are three different tests. The findings below inform the proposed Lab and its youth audience aged 16–24.

## Three insights

### 1. People can learn a specific regulation skill with feedback

The clearest example is breathing. In two college experiments, a total of 72 students practised with a VR environment that responded to respiration. Both the brief experiment and the four-week experiment found lower anxiety relative to their controls. In a separate laboratory study, people trained with a breathing game showed higher heart-rate variability (HRV) during a subsequent stress task. HRV describes variation in the time between heartbeats. The timing of the outcome matters here. Repeating a skill under a later stressor tells us more about learning than a participant feeling calmer immediately after a session. Neither design tests long-term recovery from a mental health condition at a festival. [College trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38808471/) · [Stress-transfer experiments](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.14705)

**For the road train**, begin with something a participant can recognise and repeat. A young person can practise comfortable breathing, see the effect in the environment and try the same exercise afterwards with the screen off. Measure how they feel and how well the skill transfers. Treat those as separate outcomes.

### 2. Live feedback can add value. The comparison determines how much

One of the closest experiments to the Lab gave 35 people an immersive environment responsive to breathing, heart rate and skin conductance. Both conditions helped people relax. Participants receiving their own live feedback reported greater relaxation and presence than those receiving the same kind of effects driven by someone else’s earlier recording. A different randomised study with 60 participants found that adding a pulsing heart-rate light to the same VR self-compassion exercise gave no additional benefit. In a review of six randomised breathing trials, five contributed to pooling. VR had no clear advantage over other ways of delivering breathing exercises for stress, anxiety, mood, heart rate or HRV. [Live versus replayed feedback](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2024.103275) · [Self-compassion comparison](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38896949/) · [Breathing trials review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10484-023-09611-4)

**For the road train**, test the headset-free experience on its own terms. Offer the same facilitator, music, duration and lighting design in two sessions. In one, the participant’s signal drives the environmental changes. In the other, a matched recording drives them, with participants informed about the two possible modes. This isolates the value of making the room responsive. A guided audio or ordinary-screen version offers a useful lower-cost comparison. In the dossier, “shared multimedia” describes the room. “AR” and “VR” describe studies using those particular technologies.

### 3. Shared signals can influence felt connection. Empathy and emotional intelligence need their own measures

In an experiment with pairs practising compassion meditation, sharing live respiratory or EEG feedback increased reported empathy and social presence during the session. In a public-speaking experiment with 36 people, sharing heart rates influenced the empathy that audience members reported towards the speaker. A review of 43 VR studies involving 5,644 participants found evidence for emotional empathy, with little evidence for improved cognitive perspective-taking. In a military emotional-intelligence trial, researchers tested a training curriculum and measured cortisol and performance separately. These studies cover different outcomes. A heart rhythm alone cannot tell us whether someone understands another person, feels safe or has developed a lasting skill. [Paired meditation experiment](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449358) · [Speaker–audience study](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/feeling-the-beat-enhancing-empathy-and-engagement-during-public-speaking-through-heart-rate-sharing/) · [Empathy meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/tmb0000034) · [Military emotional-intelligence trial](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-36216-8)

**For the road train**, a consented signal can change light, sound or vibration in a shared space. Resident Thrum already demonstrates that interaction with groups of two to six. The research question for the Lab is whether people subsequently report greater connection or show better perspective-taking, and for how long. Ask participants directly, and use a separate task to evaluate perspective-taking if that is an intended outcome. Leave individual emotional states private. [Resident Thrum installation](https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/27584/)

## A first test in NSW

A practical first question is whether participants aged 16–24 experience greater short-term calm during a guided road-train session when their own physiology changes the room, and whether they can repeat the breathing exercise later. Define one primary outcome before recruitment. For example, record a brief state-anxiety measure immediately before and after the session. Record breathing during a short independent practice as a separate skill outcome. Invite an optional later check-in for continued use and felt connection. Analyse immediate response, learning and follow-up separately.

Use loaned equipment and offer the same workshop without a watch. Compare the live and replayed versions in a seated workshop before moving to the main arena. First check how often the intended Apple Watch and Immersion Neuroscience / SIX setup produces a usable, timely signal at rest and during movement. Track missing data, delay and opt-outs. Physical activity changes heart rate for many reasons. Treat outputs described as Emotional Connection or Psychological Safety as model inferences and give participants a way to describe their own experience. The reviewed trials do not validate these specific scores, a crowd-wide measure of empathy, or a clinical outcome from the Lab installation. [Apple heart measurement guidance](https://support.apple.com/en-au/120277) · [Lab design record](../EMOTIONAL-LOOP.md)

For a Tanzanian or humanitarian pilot, invite local youth, refugee-led organisations and facilitators to choose the language, exercises, measures and arrangements for data. Give participants access independent of device ownership, connectivity and biometric consent. Measure who can attend, who completes follow-up, staff time, cost per session, power and offline operation. These results answer whether the road train can reach communities in material hardship. A clinical outcome in another country cannot answer that access question.

## Reading record and overlap audit

This targeted review started from the 45 submitted links. Five pointed back to a work already on the list. One was a vendor case and one was a provider directory. The remaining **38 distinct scholarly works** include empirical studies, reviews, conference contributions and a thesis. Full texts were examined for 24, abstracts for 11 and bibliographic records for three. The three record-only items are excluded from outcome claims. Workshop demonstrations, protocols, reviews and repeated reports do not represent independent clinical samples.

Discovery extended the reading list using combinations of biofeedback, HRV, respiration, mindfulness, VR, AR, mixed reality, empathy and emotional regulation with youth, military, veterans, Aboriginal communities, refugees and the route countries. The 25 selected additions include clinical evidence, comparison studies and setting-specific precedents. Primary papers and data from author repositories were preferred, including ADF research, PubMed/Europe PMC, Frontiers, Scientific Reports and US VA-hosted material. This is a targeted evidence review. A formal systematic search with independent screening and risk-of-bias scoring remains a separate exercise.

| A13 | Live versus replayed feedback | [Virtual reality experiences for breathing and relaxation training: The effects of real vs. placebo biofeedback](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2024.103275) — In a 35-person VR experiment, real-time breathing, cardiac and skin signals produced greater relaxation and presence than replayed signals from another participant. |
| A14 | Self-compassion, controlled comparison | [Receiving self-compassion in the virtual world: Testing the potential value of biofeedback](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38896949/) — Sixty people received the same VR self-compassion practice with or without a pulsing light showing their heart rate. Outcomes improved from baseline across both conditions. Adding the light produced no additional detectable benefit. |
| A15 | Empathy, shared feedback | [Augmented Virtual Reality Meditation](https://doi.org/10.1145/3449358) — Pairs practised compassion meditation with live breathing and EEG visualisations. Participants reported more empathy and social presence under shared-feedback conditions. The study measured feelings during the experience. |
| A16 | Empathy, evidence review | [Virtual reality improves emotional but not cognitive empathy: A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/tmb0000034) — Across 43 VR studies involving 5,644 participants, immersion supported emotional empathy. Cognitive perspective-taking and the added value of VR over simpler storytelling were less convincing. |
| A17 | Shared multimedia | [Resident Thrum: Designing for Group Cardiac Synchronization through Multimodal Biofeedback](https://doi.org/10.1145/3803784.3809264) — Resident Thrum translates the heart rhythms of two to six people into sound, light and vibration in a room. It establishes technical and creative feasibility. |
| A18 | Aboriginal young people, NSW | [Does Virtual Reality Training Increase Mindfulness in Aboriginal Out-of-Home Care Children?](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-024-02373-y) — A culturally informed VR mindfulness pilot enrolled 20 Aboriginal young people in out-of-home care, aged 7–16. Nineteen completed it. One mindfulness scale improved. State mindfulness and HRV showed no significant change. HRV was an outcome measure. |
| A19 | Refugee youth, Uganda | [Findings from the Tushirikiane-4-MH (supporting each other for mental health) mobile health–supported virtual reality randomized controlled trial among urban refugee youth in Kampala, Uganda](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11810762/) — In a trial with 335 urban refugee youth aged 16–25 in Kampala, the two VR-containing programmes improved self-compassion at 16 weeks. Adding a group intervention also improved mental-health literacy. Neither programme showed a depression advantage over the control. The VR programme did not use wearable feedback. |
| A20 | Refugee youth, Tanzania | [Improving mental health in low-resource settings: A feasibility randomized controlled trial of a transdiagnostic psychological intervention among Burundian refugee adolescents and their caregivers](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34392115/) — Eighty-two Burundian young adolescents and 64 caregivers took part in a feasibility trial. Trained refugee facilitators delivered the EASE programme with high fidelity. This is evidence about local delivery. |
| A21 | Refugees, Tanzania | [Evaluation of an integrated intervention to reduce psychological distress and intimate partner violence in refugees: Results from the Nguvu cluster randomized feasibility trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8213126/) — Nguvu enrolled 311 adult Congolese refugee women in Tanzania. The trial exposed barriers to access and completion. Adjusted group comparisons left the mental-health and violence outcomes uncertain. |
| A22 | Displaced Syrians, Lebanon | [Effects of a WHO-guided digital health intervention for depression in Syrian refugees in Lebanon: A randomized controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1004025) — A trial of guided digital self-help with 569 displaced Syrian adults found a depression benefit over enhanced usual care. Attrition limits interpretation. This was a guided digital programme without biofeedback. |
| A23 | Displaced people, Türkiye | [Effectiveness of a WHO self-help psychological intervention for preventing mental disorders among Syrian refugees in Turkey: a randomized controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.20939) — A trial of facilitator-led audio and illustrated self-help included 642 adults, mostly Syrian refugees. Fewer participants in the intervention arm developed a mental disorder at six months. It offers a low-equipment comparator. |
| A24 | Displaced people, direct HRV feedback | [Vagal Nerve Biofeedback Intervention for Improving Health Outcomes Among Ukrainian Forced Migrants: A Proof-of-Concept Study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12027313/) — Twenty-one Ukrainian forced migrants practised biofeedback-guided breathing. Stress and several other measures improved immediately in a before–after comparison. A controlled trial is needed to estimate the contribution of feedback. |
| A25 | Empathy, public speaking | [Feeling-the-Beat: Enhancing Empathy and Engagement During Public Speaking Through Heart Rate Sharing](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/feeling-the-beat-enhancing-empathy-and-engagement-during-public-speaking-through-heart-rate-sharing/) — In a 36-person experiment comparing real, fabricated and absent heart-rate sharing, audience members reported greater empathy towards the speaker with the system. This is a context-specific self-report. |

The 25 additions are a selected set, so their number measures the scope of this review. It is not an estimate of the total literature in any population.
